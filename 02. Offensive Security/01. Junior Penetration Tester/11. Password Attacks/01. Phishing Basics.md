## Task 1: Introduction

**Phishing** is a common technique used during penetration testing because it targets the **human element** rather than technical security controls.


### What is Phishing?

**Phishing** is an attack where an attacker ``tricks``, ``manipulates``, or ``persuades people`` into giving access or sensitive information.

A carefully crafted email can potentially:
- Bypass technical controls
- Trick users into giving up credentials
- Deliver malware
- Provide initial access to a target network



### Why Is Phishing Powerful?

Even if an organisation has strong:
- Firewalls
- Intrusion detection systems
- Other security technologies

people can still be tricked or manipulated.


### Learning Objectives
- What phishing is and its role in a pentest
- The psychology behind phishing
- Common phishing attacks
- The anatomy of a phishing campaign
- Phishing tools

## Task 2: Phishing 101


### What Is Phishing?

**Phishing** is a cyber attack that uses social engineering to trick people into revealing sensitive information or running malware.

Attackers pretend to be legitimate sources using:
- Emails
- SMS (Smishing)
- Phone calls (Vishing)
- Fake websites

The goal can be to steal credentials, gain unauthorised access, obtain sensitive data, or install malware.


#### Types of Phishing

<img width="562" height="232" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/7bc847f5-0c2f-4053-b7df-e218945d739d" />


| Type               | Meaning                                                              |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Phishing**       | Broad attack sent to many people using believable, generic messages. |
| **Spear Phishing** | Targeted and customised attack aimed at a specific person.           |
| **Whaling**        | Targeted phishing aimed at senior executives such as CEOs or CFOs.   |


#### Phishing

**Phishing** sends the **same believable message to many people**, often pretending to be an account alert, invoice, or similar routine message.

#### Spear Phishing

**Spear phishing** is **customised for a specific person**. The attacker may try to make the target click a link, open a file, perform an action, or submit credentials.


#### Whaling

**Whaling** is spear phishing that specifically targets **senior decision-makers and executives**. These people may have access to money, sensitive data, or important controls.


#### Phishing in Penetration Testing

In a penetration test, ethical hackers can use mock phishing attacks to test an organisation's resistance to social engineering.

They can measure things such as:
- Email open rates
- Link click rates
- Employee responses

This helps organisations identify weaknesses and improve their defences against phishing attacks.

## Task 3: Psychology of Phishing

**Phishing** campaigns use **social engineering** techniques to manipulate emotions and influence decision-making. These tactics often exploit vulnerabilities in human psychology to increase the likelihood of success.

> **Phishing**: When emails are sent to a target(s) purporting to be from a trusted entity to lure individuals into providing sensitive information.
>
> **Social Engineering**: The manipulation of individuals to divulge sensitive information, through various forms of communication




### Social Engineering Principles
How attackers use human psychology to influence a person's decision.

<img width="550" height="500" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/2dcc2482-dd41-4e30-b78e-899b7eaa798a" />


#### 1. Scarcity

Makes something appear limited or rare, causing the person to act quickly because they are afraid of missing out.

| Element              | Meaning                           | Example                        |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Scarcity             | Makes an opportunity seem limited | "Only three places remaining." |
| Psychological effect | FOMO / fear of missing out        | "I need to act now."           |



#### 2. Urgency
| Element | Meaning                     | Example                                       |
| ------- | --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Urgency | Creates a deadline          | "Your account will be suspended in 12 hours." |
| Goal    | Make the victim act quickly | Click the verification link                   |
| Risk    | Less time for verification  | Victim may not inspect the message            |


#### 3. Authority
Pretends to come from someone with power, ``expertise``, or an ``official role``.
| Element              | Meaning                                                     | Example                           |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Authority            | Uses a trusted position                                     | "From: IT Administrator"          |
| Common targets       | Employees                                                   | HR, IT, Finance                   |
| Psychological effect | People tend to follow instructions from perceived authority | "The IT team told me to do this." |


#### 4. Fear

Uses a threat or alarming situation to make someone react immediately.
| Element          | Meaning                         | Example                          |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Fear             | Creates anxiety or alarm        | "We detected suspicious logins." |
| Threat           | Suggests something bad happened | Account compromise               |
| Desired reaction | Fix the problem immediately     | "Secure it now."                 |


#### 5. Curiosity

Uses interesting or mysterious information to make someone want to find out more.
| Element              | Meaning                             | Example                                 |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Curiosity            | Creates an information gap          | "Confidential: Q3 roadmap highlights."  |
| Psychological effect | Person wants to know what is inside | Opens the message/file                  |
| Risk                 | Curiosity can override caution      | Victim interacts with malicious content |



#### 6. Trust
Makes the phishing message look ``familiar`` and ``legitimate``.
| Element      | Meaning                                                   | Example                             |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Trust        | Uses familiar brands, colleagues, or communication styles | "Microsoft 365 security notice"     |
| Visual clues | Logos, names, formatting                                  | Makes the message look genuine      |
| Effect       | Lowers suspicion                                          | Victim treats the request as normal |



### Cognitive Biases

The second part of the flow explains why people may fall for phishing even when they normally know about security.

| Cognitive Bias          | Meaning                                                                        | Example                                                                 |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Overconfidence bias** | Someone believes they are too smart to fall for phishing                       | "I work in cybersecurity, so I can't be fooled."                        |
| **Confirmation bias**   | Someone accepts information because it matches what they expect                | Waiting for a bank email → trusts an email claiming to be from the bank |
| **Authority bias**      | Someone trusts information because it appears to come from an authority figure | Email appears to come from a senior official                            |

## Task 4: Phishing Techniques

This section explains the **technical techniques used in phishing campaigns** to make a message look legitimate and persuade a target to interact with it.


### URL and Domain Manipulation

Attackers may manipulate URLs or domains so they appear similar to legitimate websites.
| Technique            | What it means                                                          | Example                                                       |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **URL Masking**      | A displayed hyperlink looks legitimate but leads somewhere different   | Link displays `https://tryhackme.com` but redirects elsewhere |
| **Homograph Attack** | Uses visually similar characters to make a fake domain look legitimate | `g00gle.com` instead of `google.com`                          |
| **Typosquatting**    | Registers a domain based on a common typing mistake                    | `tryahckme.com` instead of `tryhackme.com`                    |
| **URL Shortening**   | Hides the actual destination behind a shortened URL                    | Short link whose destination is not obvious                   |




#### Flow

````
Legitimate website
       ↓
Create similar-looking URL/domain
       ↓
Make the link appear trustworthy
       ↓
Victim clicks
       ↓
Victim reaches unintended destination
````



### Email Spoofing

**Email spoofing** means making an email appear to come from a trusted sender.

Using some of these techniques, we can craft an email that would look like this on the recipients end:
````email
From: Support <support@tryaccounting.thm> 
To: bob@tryaccounting.thm
Subject: Urgent: Account Verification Required

Dear Bob,

As part of our security policy, we require all TryAccounting employees to change their passwords every 3 months. Please log in to our internal portal and update your password before Friday:
http://tryaccounting-security.thm/account

Thank you,
TryAccounting Support Team
````

The visible sender information may look legitimate, but the underlying email headers can reveal a different source.
| Header / Technique | Purpose                            |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| **From**           | Displayed sender                   |
| **Reply-To**       | Address where replies are directed |
| **Return-Path**    | Mail return address                |
| **X-Sender**       | Additional sender information      |
| **Received**       | Shows mail delivery path           |


#### Important defenses

At first glance, this looks like a perfectly legitimate email. Most email clients don't show these details by default, but if we were to look at the email headers, we would see the sender's true email:
````email
From: Support <support@tryaccounting.thm>
Reply-To: attacker@phisher.thm
Return-Path: attacker@phisher.thm
X-Sender: attacker@phisher.thm
Received: from phisher.thm (mail.phisher.thm [192.168.1.25]) by mail.tryaccounting.thm
````


| Defense   | Purpose                                                             |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **SPF**   | Helps verify which servers can send mail for a domain               |
| **DKIM**  | Uses cryptographic signing to help verify message integrity         |
| **DMARC** | Defines how receiving systems should handle authentication failures |




### Credential Harvesting
Credential harvesting attempts to trick a victim into entering credentials into a fake login page.

A common example is login-page cloning.


Flow:
````
Legitimate login page
        ↓
Create a deceptive copy
        ↓
Victim visits fake page
        ↓
Victim enters credentials
        ↓
Credentials are submitted somewhere controlled
        ↓
Victim may be redirected to the real website
````

The important difference is where the submitted credentials go.

Real website: ``Browser`` → ``Legitimate authentication server``

Fake website: ``Browser`` → ``Deceptive page`` → ``Collection endpoint``




### Payload Delivery Mechanisms

The material describes a common example involving a **Microsoft Word document containing a macro**.

Sequence
````
1. Victim receives attachment
        ↓
2. Victim opens document
        ↓
3. Word asks to enable macros
        ↓
4. Victim enables content
        ↓
5. Macro executes
        ↓
6. During a real attack, malicious activity could occur
````
For a legitimate penetration test, the material explains that a benign beacon can be used instead to confirm execution without causing harm.

## Task 5: Task 5: Anatomy of a Phishing Campaign

In a professional penetration test, the goal is to test an organisation's resistance to social engineering with authorisation and without causing harm.

<img width="500" height="700" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/6d79ce15-0cbd-4701-8ba2-249eb5f0e6b6" />


### 1. Planning & Scoping

First, the pentester agrees with the client on what will be tested.

Define:
- Target user groups
- What techniques are allowed
- What techniques are not allowed
- Campaign timing
- Number of messages
- Expected outcomes
- Emergency stop/kill switch
- Authorisation and rules


Example:

| Category         | Details                                               |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| **Goal**         | Test whether employees recognise phishing emails.     |
| **In Scope**     | HR and Finance                                        |
| **Measures**     | Email opens, link clicks, and reports                 |
| **Out of Scope** | Credential collection, malware, and personal accounts |

> Planning = decide what you are allowed to test and how you will measure it.



### 2. Reconnaissance

Next, gather information about the organisation using public information.

Possible sources:
| Source              | Information                     |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Company website     | Departments, services, names    |
| Press releases      | Recent events and announcements |
| LinkedIn profiles   | Roles and departments           |
| Public social posts | Public activities               |
| News                | Recent company events           |
| OSINT               | Publicly available information  |


The goal is to make the **authorised simulation realistic**, while respecting privacy.

> Reconnaissance = collect public information to understand the target.



### 3. Scenario & Payload Development

Now use the information from reconnaissance to create a **realistic but harmless phishing simulation**.

Possible scenarios:
- Invoice reminder
- IT notification
- HR update
- Security notification
- Company announcement

For a safe penetration test, the payload should **not deploy malware** or **collect real passwords**.

Instead, we can measure actions such as:
````
Email received
      ↓
Email opened
      ↓
Link clicked
      ↓
Simulated page reached
      ↓
Event recorded
````

> Scenario development = create a believable simulation without causing real damage.



### 4. Exploitation & Post-Exploitation

This is where the authorised campaign is executed.

The source describes monitoring:
- Email opens
- Link clicks
- Simulated submissions
- Reports
- Timing
- Response behaviour

A campaign may be sent in: ``Wave 1`` -> ``Monitor results`` -> ``Wave 2`` -> ``Monitor results``

If something goes outside the approved scope, the campaign should be stopped immediately.

For safe labs, the lesson recommends using lab-safe tooling such as GoPhish or an equivalent sandboxed platform.

> Execution = run the approved simulation and measure user behaviour safely.




### 5. Reporting & Debriefing

After the campaign, analyse what happened.

Important things to examine:
| Metric                     | What it tells you                           |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Open Rate                  | How many users opened the email             |
| Click Rate                 | How many users clicked the link             |
| Credential Entry Rate      | How many attempted to submit credentials    |
| Attachment Detonation Rate | How many opened/executed an attachment      |
| Reporting Rate (24h)       | How many reported the email within 24 hours |


The report should focus on **patterns and improvements**, rather than naming individual employees.

> Reporting = explain what happened, why it happened, and how to improve security.



### Recommendations Table

these benchmarks and recommendations:

| Metric                         | What it measures                         | Benchmark                             | Recommendation                               |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **Open Rate**                  | % who opened the email                   | ~50–65% typical                       | Targeted refresher training                  |
| **Click Rate**                 | % who clicked a link                     | 8–14% acceptable; >14% high risk      | Focused security awareness training          |
| **Credential Entry Rate**      | % who entered credentials after clicking | <2% low; 2–5% moderate; >5% high risk | Phishing-site identification training, MFA   |
| **Attachment Detonation Rate** | % who opened/executed attachment         | >5–7% suggests risk                   | Safe attachment training, sandbox detonation |
| **Reporting Rate (24h)**       | % who reported email within 24h          | >40% strong; 30–40% average; <30% low | Reporting awareness campaign                 |
